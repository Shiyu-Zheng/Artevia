You can't delegate what you can't name.

3 August 2026

The admin around a music career has no agreed name, no standard list and no obvious edges. That sounds like a vocabulary problem. It's actually the reason the help doesn't get hired.

Ask a musician to write down everything they do that isn't music, and two things happen.

The first is that the list gets long. Longer than they expected, and it keeps growing for two or three days after they think they've finished — because most of it isn't stored anywhere, it's just remembered at the moment it becomes urgent.

The second is that they apologise for it. They say some of it probably isn't anyone else's job. They put question marks next to things. They organise it into categories and then explain that the categories are probably wrong.

Nobody apologises for a shopping list. The apology is the interesting part, and it points at something real.

The job has no title

Ask three musicians what the person who handles this is called and you'll get three answers. Assistant. Manager. Virtual assistant. Someone said operations manager to me recently and put a question mark after it.

Compare that with any other profession. A dentist knows they need a practice manager. A builder knows what a quantity surveyor does. Those roles have names, so they have edges, so they can be advertised for, interviewed for and priced.

The work around a music career has none of that. It has no title, so it has no scope, so there's nothing to compare a quote against and no way to tell whether you're asking for too much or too little.

Which is why nothing gets handed over

You can't search for something you can't name. You also can't brief for it. Most musicians I've spoken to have got as far as thinking I should probably get help with this and stopped there — not because of cost, but because the next step was to write a job description for a job with no name.

So the work stays where it is. And it stays in the worst possible place, which is one person's memory, competing directly with practice time.

What's actually on the list

It varies less than people expect. Written out plainly, it tends to come down to six things:

  • Getting the concert to happen — timelines, rehearsal calls, crew, who is where and when
  • Dealing with venues — availability, quotes, riders, the piano and whether anyone has tuned it
  • The inbox and the diary, and knowing what stage every thread is at
  • Somewhere for it all to live that isn't your head
  • Money out and money in, and chasing the money in
  • The things people see — the site, the poster, the programme, the bio at four different word counts

None of that is glamorous and none of it is difficult in isolation. What makes it heavy is that it never stops arriving, and that it arrives in no particular order.

The cost isn't the hours

This is the part I'd argue with most people about.

Everyone frames this as a time problem — the admin eats the practice. It does. But when musicians describe how it actually feels, the words that come up are lost, anxious and frustrated. Not busy. Those are different complaints with different solutions.

Busy is fixed by hours. Anxious is fixed by knowing — by being able to look in one place and see that the thing you half-remember at two in the morning has a date against it and somebody's name on it.

Which means the first useful thing isn't doing the work. It's writing the work down.

So write the list badly

If any of this is familiar, the exercise is worth doing on its own, with or without anyone else involved. Open a document. Write down everything that isn't music. Don't organise it, don't filter it for what seems reasonable to hand over, and don't apologise for it.

Two things usually come out of it. You find out how much of it there is, which is uncomfortable but useful. And you find out how much of it is genuinely yours — the artistic decisions, the relationships that need to be you — and how much was only ever yours because there was nobody else.

The second number is almost always bigger than people think.
